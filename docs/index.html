PV outage classifier

Finding, explaining and costing outages and faults of a PV plant at plant, inverter and string level. Seven detection methods are compared on simulated plants with known truth (a 1.25 MWp plant, 10 inverters x 12 strings, real ERA5 weather from central Poland, 12 kinds of injected events), causes are assigned by signature rules and by gradient boosting, and the result feeds availability KPIs. A second part runs the same tool on five real inverters from NREL PVDAQ.

Source code, method and data sources: github.com/kwwaleszczynski-del/pv-outage-classifier. Synthetic results are on 4 test plant-years that were never used for tuning.

4. Availability calculator

Contracts differ in what counts against the plant. Choose how each cause is treated and compare the tool's availability with the truth. Unavailable: the capacity counts as down (outage causes only) and its lost energy counts against the plant. Excluded: the period leaves the denominator (outside the operator's responsibility, unknown state). Available: the loss is a performance issue, not unavailability. The defaults follow the spirit of IEC 61724-1 and IEC TS 63019; the exact definitions need checking against the current editions and the contract.

5. Real data: five inverters of NREL PVDAQ (Las Vegas, 2016-2018)

No fault log exists, so precision cannot be measured here. What can: how often each method alarms outside the plain outages (an inverter at zero for at least two hours in sun while a neighbour produces), and whether it finds those outages. The bars use the thresholds tuned on the synthetic plants, which assume identical inverters; these five sit on different roofs, and broken clouds reach them at different times. The sweep shows what a site set-up does: the KPIs below use the lowest peer threshold that finds every plain outage at no more than one other alarm per inverter-month, with events of at least 2 h (chosen without any fault labels).

6. Real, labelled shading: twin inverters on La Réunion

A 4 kW rooftop with two identical inverters; inverter 1 was shaded on purpose on 23 days in 2022 and labelled, inverter 2 was not. With only one twin as the reference, every method trades shaded days found against alarms outside the labels. Many of those alarms are on inverter 2, on afternoons from March to September, at the same sun position day after day: the reference inverter most likely has an obstruction of its own that the dataset does not label (the classifier calls 47 of them partial shading).
